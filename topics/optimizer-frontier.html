Optimizer Frontier

The optimizer is back on the research frontier. AdamW was the unquestioned default for years, but 2024–2025 saw the first optimizers to credibly beat it at scale — most notably Muon, now used in trillion-parameter runs. This page covers Lion, Shampoo/SOAP, and Muon, with the Newton–Schulz orthogonalization at Muon's core as a runnable cell.

Prerequisite

Assumes AdamW, momentum, and decoupled weight decay from Training Dynamics & Optimizers. The organizing question here: AdamW uses a diagonal preconditioner (per-scalar adaptive LR). Every method below asks "what if we used the matrix structure of the gradient instead?"

1. Why look past AdamW

Adam rescales each parameter independently by its own gradient history — a diagonal approximation to the curvature. It ignores that a weight matrix's gradient has structure: correlations between rows and columns, an ill-conditioned spectrum. The frontier optimizers exploit that structure, either cheaply (Lion: sign) or with genuine second-order / matrix information (Shampoo, SOAP, Muon). The useful level of fluency is the one-line pitch and trade-off of each.

2. Lion — sign-based, half the memory

Lion (EvoLved Sign Momentum, discovered by symbolic program search) is the minimalist. It keeps a single momentum buffer (half of Adam's two) and its update is just the sign of an interpolated momentum — so every parameter takes a step of identical magnitude ±lr, direction only. Cheaper memory and often competitive, but the uniform step size means it needs a smaller LR and larger weight decay than Adam.

update = sign(β₁·mₜ₋₁ + (1−β₁)·gₜ)    wₜ = wₜ₋₁ − lr·(update + λw)
mₜ = β₂·mₜ₋₁ + (1−β₂)·gₜ   (one state; β₁ for the step, β₂ for the EMA)

3. Shampoo & SOAP — full matrix preconditioning

        

          Shampoo is a practical second-order method. For a weight matrix
          W ∈ ℝ^{m×n} with gradient G, it maintains two
          Kronecker-factored preconditioners — a left L = Σ GGᵀ (m×m) and a right
          R = Σ GᵀG (n×n) — and preconditions the update as
          L^{−1/4} G R^{−1/4}. This captures row/column curvature that Adam's diagonal
          misses, and converges in fewer steps — but computing matrix inverse-4th-roots periodically is
          expensive, so it's amortized over many steps and distributed across devices.
        

        

          Lₜ = Lₜ₋₁ + GGᵀ   Rₜ = Rₜ₋₁ + GᵀG    W ← W − lr · Lₜ^(−1/4) G Rₜ^(−1/4)

          SOAP (Shampoo with Adam in the Preconditioner's eigenbasis, ICLR 2025)
          stabilizes Shampoo by running Adam-style moment updates in the eigenbasis of Shampoo's
          preconditioner. It's more robust to how often you refresh the (expensive) eigendecomposition and
          reports ~15% wall-clock improvement over AdamW. The one-liner: Shampoo tells you the right basis;
          Adam adapts within it.
        


        
        
4. Muon — orthogonalized momentum (the headline)

        

          Muon (MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton–Schulz; Jordan et al., 2024) is the
          optimizer that credibly beats AdamW at scale. The idea: take the momentum matrix M,
          and orthogonalize it before stepping — replace M = UΣVᵀ with
          UVᵀ (all singular values set to 1). This is the closest orthogonal (equal-scale)
          matrix to the update, which prevents a few dominant directions from hogging the step and uses the
          full parameter space each update.
        

        

          Computing UVᵀ via SVD every step would be far too slow, so Muon uses a few
          (~5) iterations of a tuned Newton–Schulz polynomial in bf16 to approximate it.
          It applies only to 2D matrix parameters; embeddings, the output head, norms, and biases
          stay on AdamW.
        

        

          Mₜ = μMₜ₋₁ + Gₜ  →  Oₜ = NewtonSchulz(Mₜ) ≈ UVᵀ  →  W ← W − lr·(Oₜ·scale + λW) 

          NS iterate: X ← aX + b(XXᵀ)X + c(XXᵀ)²X,   (a,b,c)=(3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315)

Why Muon works & scales

          

            Intuition: Adam's diagonal scaling can let a handful of high-variance directions dominate;
            orthogonalizing the update spreads the step evenly across all directions of the weight matrix,
            effectively conditioning the update without a full second-order inverse. Empirically it reaches
            a target loss in fewer tokens — Moonshot's scaling-law study reported roughly 2×
            compute efficiency vs AdamW, and Muon powered Kimi K2 (~1T-param MoE,
            32B active, 15.5T tokens). But it does not work out of the box at scale: the next two
            sections are the recipe and the stability guard that make it production-grade.
          

        


        
        
5. The practical recipe — making Muon actually work

        

          The published playbook, converged across Jordan's original repo, Moonshot's Moonlight/K2
          reports, and independent pretraining studies:
        

        
    
          
  • Route parameters correctly. Muon takes 2D weight matrices only
            (attention projections, MLP weights). Embeddings, the LM head, RMSNorm gains, and biases go
            to AdamW — they're per-element objects where orthogonalization is meaningless. Every real
            "Muon run" is a Muon+AdamW hybrid with two param groups.
    • 
          
  • Match AdamW's update RMS with the scale rule. A raw orthogonalized update
            has RMS ≈ 1/√max(m,n) — it shrinks with matrix size, so different layers would
            take wildly different effective steps. Multiplying the update by
            0.2·√max(m,n) normalizes every matrix's update RMS to ~0.2, which is roughly
            AdamW's typical update RMS. This is the single most practical trick: it makes Muon's
            learning rate directly transplantable from an AdamW config, and it's why Moonshot
            could claim Muon works "out of the box" with their AdamW hyperparameters.
    • 
          
  • Weight decay is not optional. Early small-scale Muon skipped it; at scale,
            unconstrained weight growth eventually hurts both stability and quality. K2 used
            λ = 0.1 (decoupled, exactly as in AdamW) throughout. Moonlight's ablation calls
            weight decay one of the two changes that made Muon scale at all.
    • 
          
  • Standard knobs: momentum μ = 0.95 with Nesterov, 5
            Newton–Schulz steps, NS run in bf16 (it's robust to low precision — the
            iteration only needs to land singular values near 1, not exactly). K2's schedule:
            LR 2e-4 held constant for 10T tokens, then cosine to 2e-5.
    • 
          
  • Large-batch behavior is a feature. Independent pretraining studies
            (Essential AI) find Muon stays data-efficient at larger batch sizes than AdamW — its
            critical batch size is higher — which buys flexibility in how you parallelize a run
            (Training Dynamics §5).
    • 
          
  • Fine-tuning is the caveat. Applying Muon to an AdamW-pretrained
            checkpoint gives mixed results — the optimizer state and the loss-landscape geometry the
            weights settled into "belong" to Adam. Muon's wins are cleanest when it runs from
            initialization.
    • 
        


        
        
6. QK-clip — the stability guard that completes MuonClip

        

          Scaling Muon exposed a failure mode AdamW mostly avoids: exploding attention
          logits. The intuition follows from Muon's own design. AdamW's second-moment
          normalization automatically damps parameters with consistently large gradients; Muon
          deliberately removes that damping — every direction of the update gets full, equal
          magnitude. If some attention head's W_q, W_k are drifting toward producing huge
          q·k logits, Muon keeps pushing them there at full strength. Left alone, logits
          blow past ~1000, softmax saturates, and the loss spikes
          (Training Dynamics §3).
        

        

          QK-clip (the "Clip" in K2's MuonClip) bounds this at the weights, per
          head, after each optimizer step:
        

        

          For each head h: track Smaxh = max attention logit this step. 

          γh = min(1, τ / Smaxh)   →  
          W_qh ← √γh·W_qh,   W_kh ← √γh·W_kh
             (τ = 100 in K2)
        

        

          Since the logit is bilinear in W_q and W_k, scaling each by
          √γ scales the head's max logit by exactly γ — pinning it to τ.
          Details that matter in practice:
        

        
    
          
  • Per-head, not global. Only a small subset of heads ever explode; a global
            rescale would punish the healthy ones. Clip only the offenders.
    • 
          
  • MLA-specific handling (K2 uses multi-head latent attention): the
            head-specific components q^C, k^C get √γ_h each; the head-specific
            rotary part q^R absorbs the full γ_h; and the shared
            rotary key k^R is left untouched so one head's clip never affects the others.
    • 
          
  • It's a guard, not a controller. Clipping doesn't touch the current step's
            forward/backward — it just rescales weights so the next step can't exceed τ. In the
            K2 run, logits hit the τ=100 ceiling early, clipping was active for roughly the first 30% of
            training, then max logits decayed on their own to a healthy 10–30 range and the clip went
            dormant. Ablations showed no quality cost.
    • 
          
  • vs QK-norm: QK-norm (normalizing q and k activations) solves the same
            problem but changes the architecture and runs every step. QK-clip changes nothing in the
            model and acts only when a head actually misbehaves — which is why it could be bolted onto a
            1T-parameter run mid-design.

7. Muon at extreme scale — the distributed story

        

          The subtlety nobody hits until they shard: Newton–Schulz needs the whole gradient
          matrix of each weight, but ZeRO/FSDP shard gradients into flat, arbitrary chunks
          (Distributed §2). The production solutions:
        

        
    
          
  • Distributed Muon (Moonlight): keep ZeRO-1-style sharding, then
            all-gather each matrix's gradient shards just before its NS iteration, run NS redundantly
            or on an owner rank, and release. Communication overhead is modest because NS runs on the
            momentum, once per step.
    • 
          
  • Muon-aware bucketing (DeepSeek-V4): assign ZeRO buckets so that
            Muon-owned matrices stay gatherable as whole units, with MoE gradients communicated in
            bf16 + stochastic rounding and summed locally in fp32 — cheap comms without
            biased accumulation (Numerics).
    • 
          
  • Refined NS schedules: V4 runs a hybrid — 8 steps of the tuned quintic
            (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315) followed by 2 polish steps of (2, −1.5, 0.5) — buying a tighter
            orthogonalization when the extra matmuls are affordable. The NS cost itself is small:
            a handful of (m×n) matmuls per weight per step, in bf16.
    • 
          
  • The AdamW exception list grows with the architecture: V4 keeps embeddings,
            heads, RMSNorm gains and its mHC residual-mixing static weights on AdamW — anything
            that isn't a plain linear map stays out of Muon.

⚠️ Hybrid by construction

          

            Muon and Shampoo apply to 2D weight matrices only. Embeddings, the LM head,
            LayerNorm/RMSNorm gains, and biases are 1D (or best treated per-element) — those stay on
            AdamW. So a "Muon run" is really Muon + AdamW. Forgetting this is a common wrong answer.

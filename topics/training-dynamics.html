Topic 04 · Training science

Training Dynamics & Optimizers

The machinery that turns a good architecture and good data into a converged model. Expect the classic experiment-design probe: "the loss spiked at step 40k — what do you do?" This page rebuilds the optimizers from scratch, the schedules, the stability toolkit, and the batch-size story — each with a runnable cell.

1. Optimizers, from SGD to AdamW

Build the ladder so you can derive AdamW rather than memorize it:

  • SGD: θ ← θ − lr·g. Noisy, sensitive to LR and to the loss landscape's conditioning.
  • Momentum: accumulate a velocity v ← β·v + g, step with v. Damps oscillation across steep directions, accelerates along shallow ones.
  • RMSProp: divide by a running RMS of gradients — a per-parameter adaptive learning rate. Handles wildly different gradient scales across params.
  • Adam = momentum + RMSProp with bias correction. First moment m (mean), second moment v (uncentered variance), both EMA-smoothed and bias-corrected for their zero init.
  • AdamW = Adam with decoupled weight decay. The near-universal choice for LLMs.
m ← β₁m + (1−β₁)g    v ← β₂v + (1−β₂)g²
m̂ = m/(1−β₁ᵗ)   v̂ = v/(1−β₂ᵗ)    θ ← θ − lr·m̂/(√v̂ + ε) − lr·λ·θ

That last term − lr·λ·θ is decoupled weight decay: applied directly to the weights, not folded into g. Below, all four optimizers minimize an ill-conditioned quadratic so you can watch adaptivity + momentum earn their keep.

Why decoupled weight decay (the AdamW fix)

        

          With plain Adam + L2 (adding λθ to the gradient), the decay term gets divided
          by √v̂ just like the gradient — so parameters with large gradient history get
          less effective decay. That couples regularization to the adaptive scale, which
          isn't what you want. AdamW applies decay directly to θ, so every parameter
          decays at the same rate regardless of its gradient statistics. The cell shows the two
          diverging.

Why this matters

          

            Common openers: "Why AdamW over Adam?" (decoupled decay, above). "Why not plain SGD for
            LLMs?" (Adam's per-parameter adaptivity handles the very different gradient scales across
            embeddings/attention/FFN and is far more robust to LR). Know the typical settings:
            β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95 (LLMs often use 0.95, lower than the 0.999 default),
            ε=1e-8, wd≈0.1. Newer optimizers (Lion — sign-based, cheaper
            state; Shampoo/Muon — approximate second-order) come up as "what's new"; know the
            one-line pitch of each.
          

        


        
        
2. Learning-rate schedules

        

          The LR schedule is nearly as important as the peak LR. The standard is
          linear warmup then cosine decay. Warmup exists because Adam's second-moment
          estimate v̂ is unreliable in the first few hundred steps (tiny sample), so the
          effective step size is erratic — ramping the LR up from 0 avoids early divergence. Cosine
          then anneals smoothly toward a small final LR.
        

        

          WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay) is the increasingly popular alternative: warmup,
          a long constant phase, then a short sharp decay. Its advantage is that you can
          branch a decay from any point of the stable phase — decoupling the schedule from a fixed
          total step count, which is great for continued/uncertain-length training.

3. Stability & loss spikes

        

          The single most-asked experiment-design scenario. A loss spike is a sudden jump in training
          loss, sometimes recoverable, sometimes terminal. Build a mental decision tree of causes and
          fixes:
        

        

          

            
Likely causeSignalFix

            
              
LR too high / bad warmupspike early or after LR peaklower peak LR, longer warmup

              
A bad data batchcorrelated with a specific batch/shardskip batch, inspect/clean data

              
fp16 overflowgrad norm → inf/NaN, loss-scale thrashingswitch to bf16, loss scaling

              
Attention logit blow-uphuge attention scores, one layer divergesQK-norm, z-loss, logit soft-cap

              
Gradient explosiongrad-norm spikes above clip thresholdgradient clipping (global norm)

            
          

        

        

          Gradient clipping by global norm is the standard guardrail: compute the L2
          norm of all gradients concatenated, and if it exceeds a threshold (commonly 1.0),
          rescale every gradient by threshold / norm. This preserves direction while
          capping step size — turning a would-be catastrophic step into a merely large one.

Answering "the loss spiked, what do you do?"

          

            Don't jump to a fix. The senior answer is a diagnostic loop: (1) check whether
            it's recoverable — did loss come back down on its own? (2) correlate with the data — is it
            a specific batch/shard? (3) look at grad norm and per-layer stats — overflow, one layer,
            or global? (4) then apply the matched fix (skip batch, lower LR, clip, bf16, QK-norm), and
            (5) if terminal, roll back to the last good checkpoint and resume past the bad data with
            the mitigation in place. Mentioning checkpoint rollback + data quarantine is what
            separates the experienced answer.
          

        


        
        
4. Initialization & signal propagation

        

          Initialization controls whether activations (and gradients) grow or shrink as they pass
          through layers. Too-large init → exploding activations; too-small → vanishing. The classic
          fixes (Xavier/Glorot, Kaiming/He) scale the init variance by 1/fan_in so
          variance is preserved layer to layer. In deep transformers you also scale
          residual-branch outputs by 1/√(2·n_layers) (GPT-2 style) so the
          residual stream doesn't accumulate unbounded variance with depth.

5. Batch size, gradient accumulation, critical batch size

        

          Larger batches give lower-variance gradient estimates and better hardware utilization, up
          to a point. Two facts to hold:
        

        
    
          
  • Gradient accumulation lets you simulate a large batch on limited
            memory: run k micro-batches, sum (or average) their gradients, then step
            once. The result is identical to one big batch (for a mean loss, average the
            grads). The cell proves it.
    • 
          
  • Critical batch size (McCandlish et al.): below it, doubling the batch
            nearly halves the steps to a target loss (perfect scaling); above it, you get diminishing
            returns — more batch buys little. It grows over training as gradients get noisier
            relative to the signal. This sets the useful ceiling on DP width.
    • 
          
  • The LR ↔ batch-size relationship: when you scale batch, scale LR too —
            linear scaling as a first approximation, square-root often better for Adam. Getting this
            coupling right is a common follow-up.
    • 
        


        

          

            Gradient accumulation == large batch

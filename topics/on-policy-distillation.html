On-Policy Self-Distillation

A 2025 sweet spot between distillation and RL: let the student generate the sequences (on-policy), and have a teacher grade every token with a dense signal (reverse KL). It fixes the exposure-bias brittleness of ordinary distillation while being far cheaper than RL — and in the "self" case the teacher is the model's own stronger or earlier self, which turns out to be a clean fix for capabilities lost during fine-tuning.

Where this sits

This is the bridge between Synthetic Data (distillation) and RL for Post-training (on-policy rollouts, KL to a reference). The one-sentence positioning: on-policy sampling like RL, dense per-token supervision like distillation.

1. The two-axis map: who samples, how dense the signal

Post-training methods differ on two axes — whose trajectories you train on, and how dense the learning signal is. On-policy distillation picks the best cell of the grid.

2. Why on-policy: exposure bias & compounding errors

Ordinary distillation (and SFT) trains the student on teacher-generated transcripts — always-correct paths the student may never actually visit at inference. The moment the student makes its own mistake mid-sequence, it lands in a state the training data never covered, and errors compound. Training on the student's own rollouts puts the supervision exactly where the student actually goes, so it learns to recover from its own mistakes. (Same argument as DAgger in imitation learning.)

3. The signal: per-token reverse KL

        

          On the student's rollout, the teacher scores each token by how much the student's next-token
          distribution diverges from the teacher's. The loss is the per-token reverse KL
          — reverse because the expectation is taken over the student's distribution:
        

        

          per-token loss = KL( π_student(·|x₍₁..ₜ₎) ‖ π_teacher(·|x₍₁..ₜ₎) ) 

          = Σᵥ π_student(v)·[ log π_student(v) − log π_teacher(v) ]
        

        

          Two things make this powerful. First, it's dense: every token gets its own
          gradient signal (~N bits of information per episode), versus RL's single scalar reward
          (O(1) bit). Second, reverse KL is mode-seeking — it pushes the
          student to concentrate mass on one high-probability teacher mode rather than smearing across
          all of them, which is exactly what you want from a limited-capacity student.

Reverse vs forward KL — mode-seeking vs mode-covering

        

          Worth being able to explain on a whiteboard. Forward KL(p‖q) (expectation over
          the teacher p) is mean-seeking: q must put mass wherever p
          does, so a limited q spreads to cover all modes (and hallucinates mass in between).
          Reverse KL(q‖p) (expectation over the student q) is
          mode-seeking: q is penalized for putting mass where p is low, so it
          collapses onto a single mode. For distilling a big teacher into a smaller student, mode-seeking
          is the right bias.

4. The "self" in self-distillation

        

          Self-distillation is on-policy distillation where the teacher is the same model — a
          stronger version of itself. Common forms:
        

        
    
          
  • Earlier checkpoint as teacher — recover a capability degraded by later
            fine-tuning. This is the standout use case (below).
    • 
          
  • "Thinking" mode teaching "non-thinking" mode — distill the model's own
            chain-of-thought answers into its direct-answer mode (a technique used in the Qwen3 family) so
            the fast mode inherits the reasoning mode's quality.
    • 
          
  • Stronger sampling → cheaper policy — teacher = same model run with
            best-of-N / more test-time compute; distill that improved behavior back into a single pass
            (a self-improvement / "sharpening" loop).
    • 
          
  • EMA / mean-teacher — teacher is an exponential moving average of the
            student's own weights; the classic self-distillation stabilizer from SSL.
    • 
        


        

          
The headline result — fixing catastrophic forgetting

          

            Thinking Machines' on-policy distillation study (2025): after mid-training a model on internal
            documents, instruction-following collapsed from 85% → 45% on IF-eval (it
            learned the new knowledge but forgot how to follow instructions). On-policy distillation using
            an earlier checkpoint of the same model as teacher recovered instruction-following to
            83% while keeping the new knowledge (41% vs the pre-training 18%).
            This makes self-distillation a promising tool for continual learning:
            re-invoke lost behaviors without discarding new ones.
          

        


        
        
5. Why it's cheap — RL's credit assignment without the sparsity

        

          On-policy distillation is essentially RL where the reward is a dense, per-token teacher
          signal instead of a sparse outcome reward. That density is the whole efficiency story: the model
          learns which specific tokens were wrong, not just that the final answer was. Reported
          numbers from the Thinking Machines study:
        

        
    
          
  • Reaches teacher-level performance in 7–10× fewer gradient steps than RL,
            with an estimated 50–100× cumulative compute reduction.
    • 
          
  • AIME'24 math to ~70%: ~1,800 GPU-hours via on-policy distillation vs
            ~17,920 for the RL baseline (~9×), and ~30× vs extrapolated SFT once teacher
            FLOPs are counted.
    • 
          
  • Smaller batch sizes suffice, because each episode carries far more information.
    • 
        

        

          Contrast with GRPO: GRPO's group-relative advantage is a
          clever sparse baseline; on-policy distillation sidesteps sparsity entirely by having a
          teacher label every token. The trade-off: you need a teacher and access to its per-token
          logprobs — you can't use it where no teacher exists (that's RLVR's domain).
        


        
        
6. The training loop

        
    
          
  1. Sample a batch of completions from the current student (on-policy).
    2. 
          
  2. Score each token: run the teacher over the student's sequences to get its
            per-token distribution (or just the logprob of the sampled token, for the sampled estimator).
    3. 
          
  3. Compute per-token reverse KL as the loss/negative reward.
    4. 
          
  4. Update the student to minimize it (a policy-gradient-style update, or a
            direct supervised update toward the teacher distribution on the visited tokens).
    5. 
          
  5. Repeat — the student keeps sampling from its improving policy, so the distribution
            it's trained on tracks where it actually is.
    6. 
        

        

          
⚠️ Practical wrinkles

          

            You need the teacher's logits over the student vocab (shared tokenizer, or a mapping). Fully
            on-policy means re-sampling every step (expensive); many recipes use near-on-policy —
            a small replay of recent student samples — to amortize generation. And like all distillation,
            the student is bounded by the teacher on the distilled distribution (for self-distillation, by
            the stronger self you're distilling from).
          

        


        

          

            Exercise
            On-policy vs off-policy on a toy sequence task
            
          

          

            Build a tiny character-level task with a "correct grammar" the teacher knows. Train a student
            two ways: (a) off-policy — supervised on teacher sequences; (b) on-policy — student samples,
            teacher scores per-token reverse KL. Inject a state the teacher sequences rarely visit and show
            the off-policy student spirals there while the on-policy student recovers. This makes the
            exposure-bias argument concrete.
          

        


        

          

            Local exercise
            Recover a "forgotten" capability by self-distillation
            
          

          

            On your machine: take a small instruct model, fine-tune it hard on a narrow domain until an
            instruction-following eval regresses (reproduce the forgetting). Then run on-policy
            self-distillation with the original checkpoint as teacher (student samples on
            instruction prompts, minimize per-token reverse KL to the original) and show the eval recovers
            while the new-domain knowledge is retained. Reference: Thinking Machines, "On-Policy
            Distillation" (2025); GKD (Agarwal et al., 2023).

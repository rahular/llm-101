RL for Post-training

Post-training is where a pretrained base becomes a useful, aligned, reasoning model — and RL is the engine. The field moved fast: from PPO-based RLHF, to the offline DPO family, to critic-free GRPO and its 2025 descendants (DAPO, GSPO), to RL from verifiable rewards that produced the reasoning models. This page maps that stack with runnable DPO and GRPO cells.

Context

Post-training is a field of its own, but pre-training and post-training increasingly shape each other — you can't understand modern models without both. For a hands-on code reference, Raschka's Build a Reasoning Model (From Scratch) (ch. 6–7) implements GRPO end to end. Much of the training data here comes from Synthetic Data.

1. The post-training stack

Modern post-training is modular:

  1. SFT (supervised fine-tuning) — teach the format/instruction-following on curated (often synthetic) demonstrations. Cheap, stable, the foundation.
  2. Preference optimization — align to human/AI preferences (DPO family, or RLHF). "Make the good answer more likely than the bad one."
  3. RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) — for domains with a checker (math, code), optimize directly against correctness. This is what unlocked strong reasoning models.

The rest of the page is the "why" and "how" of steps 2–3.

2. RLHF / PPO — the original recipe

Classic RLHF has three stages: SFT → train a reward model (RM) on human preference pairs → optimize the policy with PPO against the RM, with a KL penalty to a frozen reference so the policy doesn't drift into reward-hacked gibberish. It works but is heavy: up to four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, critic/value) and notoriously finicky to stabilize.

PPO objective: maximize   𝔼[ min( rₜ·Aₜ, clip(rₜ, 1−ε, 1+ε)·Aₜ ) ] − β·KL(π ‖ π_ref)
rₜ = π(aₜ|sₜ) / π_old(aₜ|sₜ)   (importance ratio); Aₜ = advantage

3. Reward types — the quiet revolution

        
    
          
  • Outcome RM (ORM) — scores the final answer only. Simple, but sparse and
            hackable.
    • 
          
  • Process RM (PRM) — scores each reasoning step. Denser signal for
            multi-step problems, but expensive to label.
    • 
          
  • Verifiable rewards (RLVR) — skip the learned RM entirely where a
            ground-truth checker exists: run the code against tests, check the math answer. The
            reward is exact and unhackable. This shift — from imitating human preference to optimizing
            verifiable correctness — is what produced DeepSeek-R1 and the o1-style reasoning models.
    • 
        


        
        
4. DPO & the offline preference family

        

          DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) is the big simplification: a bit of algebra
          shows the RLHF objective has a closed form that lets you train directly on preference
          pairs with a simple classification-style loss — no reward model, no sampling, no RL
          loop. You just need the policy and a frozen reference. It raises the log-prob margin of
          chosen over rejected, tempered by β and anchored to the reference.
        

        

          L_DPO = −log σ( β·[ (log π(y_w) − log π_ref(y_w)) − (log π(y_l) − log π_ref(y_l)) ] )

5. GRPO — critic-free, group-relative

        

          GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; DeepSeekMath, scaled in DeepSeek-R1)
          is the method behind the recent reasoning wave. Its insight: drop the value/critic
          network (half the models, much of the instability) and estimate the advantage by
          comparing within a group. Sample G responses to the same prompt,
          score each (often with a verifiable reward), and set each response's advantage to its
          z-score within the group. Better-than-average responses get reinforced, worse-than-
          average suppressed — no learned baseline required.
        

        

          Aᵢ = (rᵢ − mean(r₁..r_G)) / std(r₁..r_G)    (advantage = reward's z-score in its group)

6. GRPO's 2025 descendants

        

          Scaling GRPO to long chain-of-thought reasoning exposed instabilities; the fixes are worth
          knowing by name:
        

        
    
          
  • DAPO (ByteDance/Tsinghua, 2025) — four tricks for long-CoT stability:
            Clip-Higher (asymmetric clip to keep exploring), Dynamic Sampling (drop
            prompts where all samples are right or all wrong — zero gradient), token-level policy
            loss (not sequence-averaged, so long responses aren't under-weighted), and overlong reward
            shaping.
    • 
          
  • GSPO (Qwen) — moves the importance ratio from token level to
            sequence level, which is more stable and better matches sequence-level rewards
            (especially for MoE).
    • 
          
  • Dr. GRPO and others — remove length/difficulty biases in GRPO's
            normalization.
    • 
        

        

          The through-line: all are critic-free, group-relative refinements chasing stability and unbiased
          gradients for long-reasoning RL.
        


        
        
7. Rollout budgets as a knapsack — difficulty-aware allocation

        

          GRPO's dirty secret: with a uniform group size per prompt, most of your rollout compute
          produces zero gradient. If all G samples succeed or all fail, every advantage in
          the group is 0 — easy prompts saturate, hard prompts never land a success, and both contribute
          nothing. DAPO's Dynamic Sampling just drops those prompts. Knapsack RL
          (ByteDance, 2025) does something smarter: reallocate the budget instead of discarding it.
        

        

          Frame each prompt's exploration as a knapsack item — cost = its rollout count
          N_i, value = the expected training signal from spending N_i samples on
          a prompt with (online-estimated) solve rate p_i:
        

        

          value(N, p) = P(mixed outcomes) × info gain 

          = [ 1 − pN − (1−p)N ] × p(1−p)²    (peaked near p ≈ 0.5, zero at p = 0 or 1)
        

        

          Maximize total value under a fixed global rollout budget — a textbook knapsack, solved with
          dynamic programming each epoch, with solve rates estimated from previous epochs' statistics
          (plus a small floor and cap per prompt). The result: easy prompts get 1–2 rollouts, prompts near
          the model's frontier get many — up to 93 in their experiments — at unchanged
          total compute. The kind of concentrated attack on a hard problem that uniform allocation could
          never afford.
        

        
    
          
  • +20–40% of gradients become non-zero during training;
    • 
          
  • +2–4 points average, up to +9 on math benchmarks vs uniform GRPO;
    • 
          
  • matching it with uniform allocation costs ~2× the compute — hence the
            paper's "computational free lunch" framing.
    • 
        


        

          

            The value function, and what allocation it implies
            
            
            
          

          
          

        


        

          This is part of a broader 2025–26 line on compute-aware RL: DAPO's Dynamic Sampling
          (drop zero-gradient prompts), Knapsack RL (reallocate by difficulty), and curriculum-style
          samplers that track solve rates to keep prompts near the model's frontier. The shared insight:
          in RLVR, the rollout budget is the scarce resource, and where you spend it is a
          first-class optimization problem — the same mindset as
          compute-optimal scaling, applied to post-training.
        


        
        
8. RL for reasoning — the R1 lesson

        

          The headline result: applying RLVR (GRPO + verifiable rewards) to a base model, with
          no reasoning demonstrations, causes long chain-of-thought and self-correction to
          emerge on their own (DeepSeek-R1-Zero's "aha moment"). RL generalizes where
          pure SFT memorizes — SFT imitates traces, while RL optimizes the outcome and discovers its own
          strategies. In practice a small SFT "cold start" is added for readability/format, then RLVR does
          the heavy lifting. This is the most important recent shift in post-training, and a likely
          discussion topic.
        


        

          
Why this matters

          

            Be ready to reason about trade-offs, not just name methods: DPO vs PPO/GRPO — offline
            & cheap & stable vs online & exploratory & able to exceed the dataset;
            why drop the critic — GRPO's group baseline removes a hard-to-train value net and its
            instability; reward hacking — the KL-to-reference penalty and verifiable rewards are
            the main defenses; ORM vs PRM — outcome is cheap/sparse, process is dense/expensive;
            why RL beats SFT for reasoning — outcome optimization + exploration vs imitation.

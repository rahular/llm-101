Topic 05 · Training science

Numerics & Precision

Low precision isn't optional at scale — but it's where silent NaNs and stalled training hide. What separates confident practitioners is precise reasoning about range vs precision, the mixed-precision recipe, and the stable formulations. Every claim here is backed by a runnable cell you can poke at.

1. The formats: range vs precision

A float is sign · mantissa · 2^exponent. The exponent bits set the dynamic range; the mantissa bits set the precision. This single trade-off explains every format choice in training.

FormatExp / MantissaMax value~Decimal digitsUse
fp328 / 23~3.4e38~7master weights, reductions
tf328 / 10~3.4e38~3matmul on tensor cores
fp165 / 1065504~3legacy mixed precision (needs loss scaling)
bf168 / 7~3.4e38~2the modern default for training
fp8 (e4m3/e5m2)4/3 or 5/2~448 / ~57344~1H100+ compute, frontier

The headline: bf16 keeps fp32's exponent (same huge range) and sacrifices mantissa. fp16 has more mantissa (more precise) but a tiny range that overflows at 65504. For training — where gradients and activations span many orders of magnitude — range matters more than precision, so bf16 wins and usually needs no loss scaling.

2. Mixed precision & master weights

        

          Mixed precision keeps a fp32 master copy of the weights for the optimizer,
          while doing the compute-heavy forward/backward in bf16/fp16. Why the master copy? Because a
          weight update is often tiny relative to the weight — and in low precision, adding a
          tiny number to a large one loses the update entirely (swamping). The fp32
          master accumulates those small updates faithfully; the low-precision copy is re-derived each
          step.

3. Loss scaling (the fp16 fix)

        

          fp16's small range cuts both ways: large values overflow, and small gradients underflow
          to zero. Activation gradients in a deep net are often below fp16's smallest normal
          (~6e-5). Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large constant
          S before backward (so all gradients scale up by S into fp16's
          representable range), then divides the gradients by S before the optimizer step.
          bf16's wider range usually makes this unnecessary — a major reason bf16 replaced fp16.

5. Determinism & floating-point non-associativity

        

          Floating-point addition is not associative: (a+b)+c ≠ a+(b+c)
          in general. So the order of a reduction changes the result at the last bits. On GPUs,
          parallel reductions use atomics and non-deterministic accumulation order, which is the root
          cause of run-to-run non-determinism. Full determinism is achievable (fixed reduction order,
          deterministic kernels) but costs performance — a real trade-off to name.

Why this matters

          

            Likely questions and the crisp answers: "bf16 or fp16 for training?" → bf16, its
            fp32-equal exponent range avoids overflow/underflow, so no loss scaling needed; fp16 is
            more precise but its 65504 ceiling is dangerous. "What does loss scaling solve?" →
            fp16 gradient underflow. "Why keep fp32 master weights?" → tiny updates get
            swamped when added to low-precision weights. "Why is my run non-deterministic?" →
            non-associative parallel reductions; deterministic mode fixes it at a speed cost.
